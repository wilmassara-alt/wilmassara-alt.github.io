5 steps to motivate Gen Z staff

Purpose, growth, flexibility, feedback and recognition. Five practical changes that keep Gen Z employees motivated and committed, from a leader who is Gen Z.

Wil Massara, Blog, Gen Z at work, published 8 October 2024, updated 24 October 2026, 4 min read

On this page

  • 1. Provide purpose and meaningful work
  • 2. Create opportunities for growth and learning
  • 3. Offer flexibility and work-life balance
  • 4. Give regular, honest feedback
  • 5. Recognise contribution publicly

Engaging Gen Z in the workplace requires a fresh approach that lines up with our values, work preferences and communication styles. For far too long we have been blaming Gen Z for being lazy, lacking work ethic or needing too much recognition. As Gen Z becomes a larger part of the workforce, organisations have to take responsibility.

Here are five things that keep Gen Z staff motivated, productive and committed.

1. Provide purpose and meaningful work

We are driven by purpose, and we want to know that our work contributes to something bigger. Offer opportunities to work on things that line up with real values: social impact, sustainability, community. This does not mean assuming what your young staff are passionate about. It means having the conversation.

Tip: Show how your company contributes to broader goals and invite young staff to be part of it. But please, no purpose-washing. Studies show young workers are significantly more likely to stay longer with employers who have a genuine social purpose. They can also smell a fake one from the car park.

2. Create opportunities for growth and learning

Gen Z wants continuous learning and professional development. Provide clear pathways, mentoring and access to skill-building. If the next step is invisible, assume your best young staff are already looking elsewhere.

Tip: Build a 90-day plan with every new hire under 25 that names the skill they’ll have at the end of it. Then actually review it.

3. Offer flexibility and work-life balance

Flexibility is not a perk to us. It’s the baseline. Remote and hybrid options, flexible hours and trust about where the work gets done all signal respect.

Tip: Measure output, not hours at a desk. The team member who finishes at 3pm because the work is done is not a problem to fix.

4. Give regular, honest feedback

We grew up with instant feedback on everything. An annual review is an eternity. Short, regular, specific conversations beat a formal appraisal every time.

Tip: A ten-minute weekly check-in with two questions: what’s working, what’s in the way. That’s it.

5. Recognise contribution publicly

Recognition is not about trophies. It’s about being seen. Call out good work in front of the team, credit ideas to the person who had them, and let young staff present their own work to leadership.

Tip: If a young team member’s idea gets adopted, they present it. Not their manager.

None of this is complicated. It is just different to how most workplaces were set up. The question is whether you’d rather change the environment or keep re-hiring for the same role every 14 months.
