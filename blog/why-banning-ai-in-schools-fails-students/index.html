Why banning AI in schools fails students

Schools that ban AI are not protecting students. They are sending them into a workforce that has already changed.

Wil Massara, Blog, AI in education, published 29 September 2026, 5 min read

  • What does a ban actually teach students?
  • What should schools do instead of banning AI?

Banning AI in schools fails students because it removes the one place they could learn to use it well. It does not stop students using AI. It stops them being taught.

Last year I said something that faced some backlash. Schools and our education system must embrace AI. Every month since, the evidence that this is true has got louder.

AI is not coming. It’s well and truly here.

Why is banning AI in schools the wrong call?

Because the ban only works inside the school gate, and students do not live inside the school gate.

When Atlassian announced it was cutting 1,600 jobs because of AI, the headline should have made every school leader pause. Not panic. Pause. That is the labour market Year 10 students are walking into in a few short years.

The DELL Future of Work report estimates 85% of the jobs that will exist in 2030 have not been invented yet. We cannot teach students the job. We can teach them the tools and the judgement. A ban teaches them neither.

Here’s the truth imo: pretending students will “figure it out later” is the worst thing a school can do. Later is when it counts, and nobody will be there to coach them.

What does a ban actually teach students?

Three things, none of them intended.

That AI is cheating. So students use it in secret, badly, with no idea where it is wrong. The students who get caught are the ones who did not learn to hide it. The ones who did learn to hide it learned the wrong lesson entirely.

That school and the real world are different places. I speak to tens of thousands of students a year and this gap is the thing they feel most sharply. They know what the tools can do. They watch adults around them use them. Then they walk into a classroom where the tools are banned and the work is designed as if 2019 never ended.

That the adults are behind. Harsh, but that is how it lands. Trust in a school drops when students believe the rules are written by people who do not understand the thing being banned.

What should schools do instead of banning AI?

Three moves, all possible this term.

1. Be an advocate for AI in your school and your system. Not a cheerleader. An advocate. Someone who says out loud that students will be assessed, hired and judged in a world with AI in it, and that the school’s job is to prepare them for that world rather than a previous one. This is leadership work, and it starts with the principal.

2. Double down on human skills. The more AI can write, summarise and calculate, the more valuable the things it cannot do. Leading a team. Asking a better question. Standing in front of a room. Disagreeing without damage. These are leadership skills, and they are exactly the ones most schools still leave until Year 12 or leave out. AI makes the case for leadership as a core skill stronger, not weaker.

3. Encourage curiosity about the technology. Let students break it. Let them find where it is wrong. Make “show me how you used AI and where it failed you” part of the assessment. Visible use beats hidden use every time.

How should a school write its AI policy?

With students in the room.

A policy written about young people without young people is tokenistic, even when it is well-intentioned. On AI it is also just worse, because the students are often the most experienced users in the building.

Put a draft policy in front of a mixed group of students and staff. Ask narrow questions. Which rules would you follow? Which would you route around? What do you wish a teacher had told you a year ago? Then change the draft and show them what changed.

A school that does this ends up with a policy students recognise as theirs. A school that bans AI and emails the policy home ends up with a policy students have already found a way around.

Isn’t this risky?

Yes. So is the alternative.

The risk of embracing AI is that some assessment gets messier for a couple of years while schools redesign it. The risk of banning it is a cohort that leaves school with no guided experience of the most significant workplace shift in a generation, and finds out on day one of their first job.

One of those risks is recoverable. One is not.

I have a lot of respect for teachers and school leaders. This is not a criticism of them. It is a criticism of a system that moves slowly by design and is now facing something that does not. Working with the system means saying the uncomfortable thing early, and this is it.

What is your school’s AI policy, and did a single student help write it?

Quick answers

Should schools ban AI?

No. Banning AI in schools removes the one place students could learn to use it well, with guidance. It does not stop them using it. It stops them being taught.

What should schools do about AI instead of banning it?

Teach it openly, redesign assessment so AI use is visible rather than hidden, double down on the human skills AI cannot replace, and involve students in writing the school's AI policy.

Why does AI matter for school students specifically?

The DELL Future of Work report estimates 85% of the jobs that will exist in 2030 have not been invented yet. Students in Year 7 today will enter that workforce. AI fluency will be assumed, not taught, by the time they get there.
